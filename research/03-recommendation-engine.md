# Steer practice quietly toward weak topics

> **What was built (14 Nov 2026, ADR 0005).** Ideas #1, #2, #4, #5 and #7 shipped as the "smart mix". Two corrections to the brief this research was based on:
> - **#6 was already true.** Every answered question, correct ones included, already gets an FSRS card (ADR 0004). The brief wrongly said only wrong or skipped answers did.
> - **#3 was rejected.** About 89% of MedMCQA explanations name the answer letter ("Ans. b."), so shuffling options would contradict them.
>
> #8 (re-asking a missed question in the same session) wasn't built: FSRS already brings it back the next day. The other ideas are deferred; see docs/NEXT.md.

MedMinutes should keep FSRS-6 for timing reviews and add a small, explainable "smart mix" layer that picks *new* questions. Doing this automatically is a real gap in the NEET PG market: Marrow, PrepLadder and others make the student build "incorrect / unattempted / bookmarked" modules by hand. The cheapest wins need no new model. Order reviews by lowest **retrievability** (R, FSRS's estimated chance you still remember a card) instead of days overdue. Shuffle answer options. Compute a per-topic "weakness" score with **shrinkage** (pulling small-sample estimates toward a sensible average) from first attempts only. Then fill each session with a capped share of due reviews, mostly weak-topic new questions, some coverage questions and a few purely random ones, interleaved so that no more than about one in three or four questions is "likely wrong". The science behind this is strong for spacing, retrieval practice and corrective feedback. It is moderate for interleaving similar topics. The exact percentages, the 70–75% target success rate and the topic-weakness thresholds are design choices nobody has validated for medical MCQs, so build them as tunable settings and log every pick from day one. Deep-learning "knowledge tracing" is the wrong tool at MedMinutes' scale. With about 180k questions and few users, simple logistic or Elo models beat deep models in published benchmarks, and they run free in Postgres and TypeScript.

> **How to read the evidence labels.** "Proven" means meta-analyses or several controlled experiments. "Supported" means one good study, a strong benchmark, or a documented product practice. "Inference" means a reasoned design choice that nobody has tested for this setting. Claims the researchers could only see in search-engine snippets, or did not re-check against the paper, are flagged as **(unverified)**.

## Indian qbanks leave the adapting to the student

The NEET PG apps the researchers checked publicly offer **user-driven filters**, not documented adaptive engines. PrepLadder's settings filter by Bookmarked, Incorrect, Previously Unanswered and Previously Attempted questions, plus topic and tags ([PrepLadder guide](https://www.prepladder.com/fmge-study-material/preparation-strategy/prepladder-reinvented-user-guide-your-guide-to-uninterrupted-learning)). Marrow's Custom Modules filter by difficulty, subject, tags, bookmarks and incorrect answers, and since the 2026 pattern change they support 36-question "Exam Mode" sets (unverified, snippet) ([Marrow blog](https://www.marrow.com/blog/neet-pg-2026-new-exam-pattern-and-how-marrow-is-adapting/)). Marrow's "Magic Module" is the only adaptive claim found. The line that it "intelligently prioritizes your weak areas" comes from a third-party app listing, not from Marrow, and is **marketing, not documentation** ([mwm.ai listing](https://mwm.ai/apps/marrow-for-neet-pg-next/1226886654)). No selection logic was found for Cerebellum, DAMS/eMedicoz, BoardVitals or Osmosis. UWorld also works on filters (Unused, Incorrect, Marked, Custom) ([UWorld nursing page](https://nursing.uworld.com/?p=23441), snippet, NCLEX version). Its newer feature lets students build a test straight from their Reports page ([UWorld blog](https://medical.uworld.com/blog/product-updates/creating-a-test-from-the-reports-section/), title only).

Only a few products publish real engineering, and they share a pattern. Duolingo's **Birdbrain** estimates learner ability and exercise difficulty together to predict P(correct), the chance of a right answer, and serves exercises at "just the right difficulty". Duolingo says A/B tests showed learners "learn more", but published no numbers ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/learning-how-to-help-you-learn-introducing-birdbrain)). It first ran in **shadow mode**, logging its predictions without acting on them, before going live ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo)). **AMBOSS**, which now markets a NEET PG product in India ([AMBOSS NEET PG](https://www.amboss.com/int/in/neet-pg)), runs Adaptive Question Sessions. According to a search snippet, each pick is followed by simulating "as if the user answered correctly", so the next pick moves on rather than hammering the same gap. Its Knowledge Profile whitepaper says a session opens on the learner's weakest area (both unverified: the help page returned 403 and the PDF could not be parsed) ([AMBOSS help](https://support.amboss.com/hc/en-us/articles/45511273700369); [whitepaper](https://blog.amboss.com/hubfs/Knowledge%20Profile%20Whitepaper%20-%20Final%20preprint%20version%20(July%202026).pdf)). **Brainscape** holds back new material until enough low-confidence cards are upgraded ([Brainscape CBR](https://m.brainscape.com/academy/confidence-based-repetition-definition)). **Khan Academy** uses visible mastery levels (Attempted → Familiar → Proficient → Mastered) that can go down after mixed quizzes ([Khan Help](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/tr/articles/5548760867853)).

The takeaway for MedMinutes: estimate P(correct) from learner ability and item difficulty, aim near a target band, add a diversity step so one session doesn't fixate on a single topic, and launch in shadow mode. All of that copies proven industry practice. One gap remains: **no medical qbank publishes an effect size for its adaptive mode**, so nobody can show that adaptive selection beats filters for NEET PG scores.

## Memory science supports hidden spacing, testing and mixing, not exact ratios

Two techniques have the strongest evidence of any study method: **spacing** (spreading practice out over time) and **retrieval practice** (testing yourself rather than rereading). Dunlosky's 2013 review rated them the only two "high utility" techniques out of ten ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266); DOI from the researcher's prior knowledge). A 2021 meta-analysis found spaced versus massed *retrieval* practice at **g = 0.74**. (g, or Hedges' g, is a standardised effect size: 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.) The same analysis found **expanding versus equal gaps made essentially no difference (g = 0.034, not significant)** ([Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)). So MedMinutes should not engineer special expanding schedules. Spacing at all, plus personalisation, is where the gain is. Personalisation itself has an RCT behind it. Lindsey and colleagues' model-based scheduler gave **+16.5% retention over massed study and +10% over one-size-fits-all spacing** on a cumulative exam a month after a semester-long course ([Lindsey et al. 2014](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Research/Selected%20Publications/reprints/LindseyShroyerPashlerMozer2014Published.pdf)). Cepeda's 1,350-person study adds a useful rule: the best gap between reviews is roughly **20–40% of a one-week horizon but only 5–10% of a one-year horizon**, and gaps that are too short hurt more than gaps that are too long ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)). That is the scientific basis for making scheduling aware of the exam date.

Retrieval practice beats rereading by about **g ≈ 0.50** overall ([Rowland 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/)). Multiple-choice was the most effective single practice-test format in Adesope's 272-effect-size meta-analysis, with an overall effect of about g ≈ 0.61 ([Adesope et al. 2017](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/); partly verified). Rowland's feedback figures (g 0.73 with feedback, 0.39 without) come from secondary sources and are **unverified**. The result also holds in medicine. Residents who did repeated testing with feedback scored **39% versus 26%** for repeated study more than six months later (**d = 0.91**) ([Larsen et al. 2009](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2023/3/30)). The known risk of MCQs is that students remember the wrong options they picked ([Roediger & Marsh 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.5.1155); prior knowledge). **Showing the correct answer neutralises most of that risk**, whether the feedback is immediate or delayed ([Butler & Roediger 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18491500/)). MedMinutes already reveals answers, so its core loop is sound. Two follow-ons matter for selection. A wrong answer deserves a re-test soon. And errors made with high confidence are corrected well at first but are **more likely to come back a week later** ([Butler, Fazio & Marsh 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21989771/)), which argues for an optional "I was sure / I guessed" tap.

**Interleaving** means mixing topics instead of doing one topic in a block. It has a medium overall effect (**g = 0.42**), and it works best when categories are *similar and easy to confuse*: nephritic syndromes, antiarrhythmics, look-alike organisms ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)). Learners consistently *believe* blocking works better even after interleaving helped them ([Kornell & Bjork 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x); prior knowledge). That is the strongest scientific argument for doing this "without the user noticing". Learners' own choices are systematically worse than what helps them ([Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell 2013](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823)), so the system should control the mix and order while the user still chooses the subject.

On **success rate**, the evidence is softer than its reputation. The "85% rule" was derived for gradient-descent machine learners on binary tasks, **not shown in human memory**, and its authors did not recommend it as a grade target ([Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)). The best human data come from a geography practice system with 1.7M answers. Students rated questions "appropriate" at about a **65% real success rate**, and "too easy" equalled "too hard" there ([Papoušek & Pelánek 2015](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/aied15.pdf)). A follow-up found easy questions better for short-term engagement and harder ones better for long-term learning (abstract only) ([Papoušek et al. 2016](https://is.muni.cz/publication/1350112/en/Impact-of-Question-Difficulty-on-Engagement-and-Learning/Papousek-Stanislav-Pelanek?lang=en)). So a starting target of **70–75% predicted correct**, nudged by the user's last ten answers, is defensible but remains a setting to tune. For "when is it learned", the medical spaced-education system **retired an MCQ after two consecutive correct answers** ([Kerfoot et al. 2012](https://www.evl.uic.edu/datsoupi/420/docs/An-Online-Spaced-Education-Game-to-Teach-and-Assess-Medical-Students.pdf)). The popular "3 correct, then relearn 3 times" rule from Rawson and Dunlosky appears only in a secondary summary (**unverified**) ([Memory-Key summary](https://www.archive.memory-key.com/research/news/testing-learn-best-practice?page=1)). Because a four-option guess succeeds 25% of the time, one correct MCQ is weak evidence, and the "two in a row" rule fits better.

## FSRS-6 is the right memory engine; fix what feeds it and how it orders

There is no reason to switch algorithms. On the open benchmark (about 10k Anki users, about 350M reviews), FSRS-6 with fitted parameters scores **log loss 0.3460 / AUC 0.7034**, against DASH at 0.3682 / 0.6311, HLR (Duolingo's half-life regression) at 0.4694 / 0.6369 and ACT-R at 0.4033 / 0.5225 ([srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)). Log loss measures how well-calibrated the predictions are (lower is better). AUC measures how well the model ranks remembered above forgotten cards (higher is better; 0.5 is a coin flip). FSRS-7 and neural models do somewhat better, but ts-fsrs v6 needs Node 24 ([ts-fsrs README](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/ts-fsrs)). The researchers confirmed locally that ts-fsrs 5.4.2 already runs the FSRS-6 model. Binary Again/Good grading is officially fine: "FSRS may even be more accurate if you only use Again and Good" ([Anki FSRS FAQ](https://docs.ankiweb.net/faqs/frequently-asked-questions-about-fsrs.md)). One caveat: the benchmark uses self-graded Anki flashcards, and **nobody has tested how well it transfers to objectively graded MCQs with guessing**.

The first concrete fix is **review order**. "Most overdue first" counts days late, so it favours long-interval cards that are probably still well remembered. For backlogs, Anki recommends **ascending retrievability**, which shows the cards you are most likely to have forgotten first ([Anki deck options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html)). The MEMORIZE paper's optimal rule, "review intensity grows with forgetting probability", points the same way ([Tabibian et al. 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6410796/)). FSRS-6's curve R = (1 + factor·t/S)^(−decay), with decay 0.1542 by default, can be computed in SQL from `stability` and `last_review`. Anki presents this as a recommendation, not a tested result: **no trial compares sort orders on learning outcomes**.

The second fix is **what becomes a card**. Today only wrong or skipped questions get cards. That throws away information and never re-checks lucky guesses. Under a simple guessing model, if you were 50/50 on knowing an item, a correct four-option answer means you knew it only about 80% of the time (an analytical inference in the notes). The FSRS-consistent design creates a card on every first attempt. A correct answer is graded Good, giving stability S ≈ 2.31 days and a first review in about 3 days with current settings (measured locally). Because `review_cards` is rebuilt from `attempts`, this is a replay with `npm run srs:rebuild`, not a data migration. It will enlarge the review queue a lot. That makes the third fix necessary: **throttle new questions when the backlog grows**. Reddy and colleagues found a **sharp phase transition**, where learning collapses once new items arrive faster than a threshold ([Reddy et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07032)). Brainscape and Anki apply the same idea in their products.

The fourth fix is **exam-date awareness**. FSRS aims for 90% retention indefinitely. It does not know that NEET PG is a fixed date. Combining Cepeda's findings with FSRS gives a cheap recipe, which is **inference, not a tested scheduler**. Store each user's exam date. Cap `maximum_interval` at the days remaining. Raise `request_retention` in steps as the exam nears (for example 0.85 → 0.90 → 0.93). In the last one to two weeks, review the cards with the lowest predicted R *on exam day*. Anki's docs warn that above 90% retention "the workload increases very quickly" ([Anki deck options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html)), so the step values need care. Later, once a few thousand pooled reviews exist, a free GitHub Action can fit one MedMinutes-wide FSRS parameter set with the Python optimizer. Adopt it only if it beats the defaults on a time-ordered holdout. The researchers did not verify the Python package name, and no source gives a reliable minimum number of reviews for a per-user fit.

Response time (already stored as `attempts.time_taken_ms`) should **not** become a Hard/Easy grade yet. FSRS ignores time ([Anki FSRS FAQ](https://docs.ankiweb.net/faqs/frequently-asked-questions-about-fsrs.md)), and no study shows that "slow correct" means "less stable" for MCQs. Keep it as a logged feature to test later.

## A tiny learner model beats deep learning at MedMinutes' scale

FSRS answers "when should this card come back?" It cannot answer "which of 180k unseen questions next?" That job needs a **learner model**: a small model that predicts P(correct) for a user on an unseen question and ranks topics by weakness. The best benchmark (9 datasets) found that a well-featured logistic regression ("Best-LR": user ability, item difficulty and log-scaled counts of past right and wrong answers, overall and per topic) **beats Deep Knowledge Tracing when there are few learners per item**. LR won where the median was 1–10 learners per item, and DKT won only at roughly 22–137 learners per item or tens of thousands of users ([Gervet et al. 2020](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/451)). Classic Bayesian Knowledge Tracing lagged behind both. Fancier attention models added little once evaluation was standardised ([pyKT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.11460); per-model tables not extracted). MedMinutes will have 0–5 learners on most items for a long time. That puts it deep in logistic/Elo territory, and deep models would also break the ₹0 budget.

**Elo** is the practical engine. It is the chess rating idea applied to students and questions: P(correct) = 0.25 + 0.75·σ(θ_user − d_item), where σ is the logistic function and 0.25 is the four-option guessing floor. After each answer, the user's ability θ moves up and the item's difficulty d moves down by K·(result − P). K shrinks with experience via U(n) = a/(1+b·n), and "a = 1, b = 0.05" is suggested as a starting point ([Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf)). It needs one number per user (or per user and subject) and one per item, no offline training, and one UPDATE per answer. Two caveats from the same source shape the design. Elo needs about **100 students per item** for good item difficulties, so new items should start at their topic's average difficulty. And plain "% correct" estimates difficulty well **only while selection is random**. Once selection becomes adaptive, % correct becomes biased and Elo is needed.

For **"is this topic weak?"**, use a **Beta-binomial with shrinkage**: p_topic = (correct + m·p_subject) / (attempts + m), with m ≈ 5–10 pseudo-attempts. This stops one wrong answer out of two from flagging a topic ([ebbr / empirical Bayes](https://rdrr.io/github/dgrtwo/ebbr/f/README.Rmd)). Count **first attempts only**, because FSRS re-reviews of a missed item would inflate the wrong count. Call a topic weak only when the evidence is confident, for example P(true accuracy < target) > 0.8. The researchers found **no study validating any particular m or threshold**, so these are design choices.

Choosing items is then a scoring function. Papoušek and Pelánek's system scored each candidate on closeness to the target P, a recency penalty (−1/seconds since last seen, weighted 12) and a novelty term (1/√(1+times seen)). They moved the target based on the last ten answers. In a randomised test with more than 30,000 answers, students answered a **median of 33 questions with adaptive selection versus 19.5 with random selection** ([Papoušek & Pelánek 2015](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/aied15.pdf)). That is an engagement result, not a learning result, and the fully random arm had only 26 students (p = 0.06). A cheaper two-step version fits MedMinutes. First pick a topic, weighted by weakness and optionally by exam weight. Then pick an unseen item in that topic whose predicted P is closest to the target. Bandit methods such as **ZPDES**, which rewards topics where the success rate is *improving*, beat an expert sequence for 400 schoolchildren in 3 of 4 exercise types ([Clément et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.3174)). They suit the topic level, not 180k items. **Thompson sampling** over topic Beta posteriors (draw a plausible accuracy for each topic and pick the lowest) is a natural fit, but it is the researcher's own inference, with no published evaluation.

**Exam weighting** is weakly grounded. NBEMS last published a subject split in 2019 (Medicine 45, Surgery 45, OBG 30, Pathology 25, PSM 25, and so on, out of 300, labelled "indicative and purely provisional"). The 2026 bulletin has none, and the paper is now 180 questions ([Oncourse summary](https://getoncourse.ai/neet-pg/subject-wise-weightage/)). Any weighting must be labelled approximate.

Finally, evaluation needs planning *before* launch. Adaptive data creates a feedback loop: "even a simple baseline model achieves a good predictive accuracy" on adaptively collected data, which hides real differences. The fix is **controlled randomisation** ([Pelánek 2017](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-overview.pdf)). A small share of uniformly random picks, logged together with the policy that chose them, later allows **replay evaluation**, an unbiased offline test of a new policy on old logs ([Li et al. 2011](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1003.5956)). It is also what keeps the syllabus covered.

## Ranked ideas: impact versus effort

Ranking puts expected learning impact per unit of build effort first, then how solid the evidence is. Effort assumes the existing stack (Drizzle, ts-fsrs 5.4.2, `attempts` → `review_cards` replay).

Two things are deliberately *off* the list. **Deep knowledge tracing** loses to logistic models at this data scale and costs money. **Changing sibling cards' FSRS state** when a related question is answered is also excluded: FSRS was not fitted with that coupling, and it would break replay determinism. Topic transfer is real (d ≈ 0.40 for transfer of test-enhanced learning ([Pan & Rickard 2018](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018.pdf))), but it belongs in *selection*, not in card state.

## Build in four phases, measuring before steering

**Phase 1, groundwork (about a week, no visible change).** Add the selection logging (#2), the retrievability ordering (#1) and option shuffling (#3), plus the topic-weakness query (#4) shown only on the dashboard. All schema changes are additive, so they are safe on the shared Neon database. This phase also starts collecting unbiased data, because new-question selection is still random within the chosen topic, which is exactly when raw % correct is a fair difficulty estimate.

**Phase 2, the smart mix (one to two weeks).** Ship the session builder (#5) with in-session re-testing (#8) and the backlog throttle (#7). Start in **shadow mode**: compute and log what the smart mix *would* have served while still serving random questions, then switch on for the owner's account first. Keep the 5–10% random slots permanently.

**Phase 3, memory upgrades (one to two weeks).** Create cards for every first-seen question (#6), add the exam date (#10) and the optional confidence tap (#11). Each FSRS change needs `npm run srs:rebuild --dry-run` and then the rebuild, and should go into an ADR because it changes ADR 0002/0004 behaviour.

**Phase 4, calibration (once data exists).** Add Elo difficulty and ability with a target-P term (#9), duplicate grouping (#12), and the nightly GitHub Actions jobs that compare % correct, Beta, Elo and logistic models on time-ordered holdouts and fit pooled FSRS parameters (#13). Run a user-level A/B test (by hashing the user id) of smart mix versus random. With a small user base, statistical power will be low. Choose one or two primary metrics up front, such as accuracy on random "probe" questions from previously weak topics and seven-day return rate.

## Open decisions for the owner

These need a choice before building. **Scope:** should the smart mix stay inside the subject/topic the user picks (least surprising), or should there also be an "All subjects, smart mix" button? **Card policy:** do you accept a much larger review queue in exchange for re-checking correct answers (#6)? If yes, should correct first answers start as Good (3-day first review) or Easy (8-day), pending real data? **Visibility:** the evidence favours *silent* mixing during practice (learners rate effective difficulty as worse), with weak topics named only on the dashboard. Is that the experience you want, or do you prefer Khan-style visible mastery levels? **Target success rate:** start at 70% or 75%? **Exam date:** ask every user for their NEET PG date at sign-up or in settings? **Skips:** NEET PG scores a skip as 0, not −1, yet FSRS treats it like a wrong answer. Keep that, or treat skip as "unsure" for topic weakness? **Exam weights:** use the 2019 provisional NBEMS split, or leave weighting out until better data exists?

## Conclusion

The strongest finding is that MedMinutes' best next step is *plumbing and policy*, not a smarter model. FSRS-6 already beats the classic models, and at MedMinutes' data scale even simple Elo matches or beats deep models. What is missing is a well-mixed, interleaved, success-rate-aware **session** built from cheap per-topic statistics, plus the logging and random slots that make it measurable. The evidence also explains why "without the user noticing" works in the learner's favour, not just as a UX trick: students reliably misjudge interleaving and difficulty, so hiding the steering removes a bias rather than adding one.

The honest uncertainty is in the numbers, not the direction. Spacing, retrieval with feedback and personalised scheduling are proven. The 70–75% target, the session ratios, the weakness thresholds, the exam-date retention steps and "card for every question" are all reasoned guesses for medical MCQs. Treat them as settings with logs attached, and MedMinutes can produce the effect sizes that no NEET PG competitor has published.
